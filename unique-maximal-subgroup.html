Unique Maximal Subgroup

Group Theory • Source: Algebra: A Graduate Course by I. Martin Isaacs
Problem: Let $G$ be a finite group such that it has a unique maximal subgroup. Prove that $G$ has prime power order.

My Initial Thoughts

At first, I thought of using an induction argument. If we let $H$ be the unique maximal subgroup, then $H$ must be normal and its index is prime. I even considered using Zorn's Lemma to invoke the fact that every proper subgroup is contained in a maximal subgroup. However, since $G$ is finite, Zorn's Lemma is entirely unnecessary! I later realized that this is actually an easy problem if you break it down into two simple parts.

The Proof

Part 1: Prove that $G$ is cyclic.

Let $H$ be the unique maximal subgroup of $G$. Since $H$ is a proper subgroup, there exists some element $g \in G$ such that $g \notin H$. Consider the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$, denoted $\langle g \rangle$.

If $\langle g \rangle$ were a proper subgroup of $G$, it would have to be contained in some maximal subgroup. But since $H$ is the only maximal subgroup, we would have $\langle g \rangle \subseteq H$, which immediately implies $g \in H$. This contradicts our initial assumption that $g \notin H$.

Therefore, $\langle g \rangle$ cannot be a proper subgroup. It must be that $\langle g \rangle = G$. Hence, $G$ is cyclic.

Part 2: Prime Divisors of the Order of $G$

Now that we know $G$ is cyclic, we can use the fundamental theorem of cyclic groups. If there exist two distinct prime divisors of the order of $G$, say $p$ and $q$, then there must exist a unique subgroup of index $p$ and a unique subgroup of index $q$.

Since subgroups of prime index are maximal, $G$ would have at least two distinct maximal subgroups. But we are given that $G$ has a unique maximal subgroup. Thus, it is impossible for the order of $G$ to have more than one distinct prime divisor.

Conclusion: The order of $G$ must be a prime power.
