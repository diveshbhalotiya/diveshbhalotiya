Sources

A curated bibliography of the textbooks, lecture notes, and references that have shaped my understanding of mathematics.

Abstract Algebra

  • Algebra
    by Michael Artin
    This one has a great quality of exercises, and I personally like the way he introduces quotient rings. It is an excellent book for abstract algebra.
  • Abstract Algebra
    by David S. Dummit and Richard M. Foote
    I would say this has more exercises compared to other books, and for the exercises, the quality is on par with Artin. However, it introduces you to many more topics that are not covered in Artin.
  • A First Course in Abstract Algebra
    by John B. Fraleigh
    Some of the problems in this book gave me valuable tools to tackle other mathematical problems.
  • Algebra: Chapter 0
    by Paolo Aluffi
    I don’t suggest this for beginners or those who do not know category theory. But I can say if anyone knows a little bit of category theory, it is a very good choice for a secondary course.
  • Algebra: A Graduate Course
    by I. Martin Isaacs
    I am currently working through this and I can say it is one of the books I should have done earlier in my undergraduate studies. It has a great quality of problems.

Metric Spaces

  • Real Analysis
    by N.L. Carothers
    An excellent book that is highly rich in exercises. I personally learned a tremendous amount about metric spaces from this text.
  • Principles of Mathematical Analysis
    by Walter Rudin
    Of course. A definitive classic.
  • Set Theory and Metric Spaces
    by Irving Kaplansky
    I am currently going through this, and it is an excellent book. If you truly want to understand metric spaces from the ground up, I highly recommend going through it.

General Point Set Topology

  • Topology
    by James R. Munkres
    The standard textbook for the subject. If you really want to do rigorous mathematics, you must go through it. When I first saw the definition of a topology, I didn't understand what we were doing—which I think is very natural for beginners. Consistency and persistence are key here.
  • Topology Without Tears
    by Sidney A. Morris
    A great resource for beginners, but its main drawback is that it can become a way to escape the rigorous mathematics found in Munkres. I personally used this for my undergraduate course, but later realized that Munkres is ultimately the standard I needed to master.
  • General Topology
    by Ryszard Engelking
    I am trying to go through this whenever I have time. It is quite terse, but it provides a massive amount of results that you can easily generalize further.
