The Klein Bottle and the Patience for Mathematics

October 13, 2026

Today I had an exam for my Algebraic Topology course. In my defense, I hadn't done as many exercises as I probably should have beforehand, but I learned an immense amount during my preparation.

During the exam, there was one particular question asking to find the fundamental group of the Klein bottle. I ended up spending almost an hour wrestling with it. After the exam, I checked my work and realized my approach wasn't even close to the actual solution.

But despite that, I really enjoyed the experience. I had already seen the remaining questions on the exam before, so this was the only one that was completely new to me. That novelty is exactly what made it so interesting. I tried multiple different ways to solve it from scratch. In this current era of AI, where quick answers are always immediately available, I genuinely appreciated the feeling of spending an uninterrupted hour focused on a single problem.

One might ask: why don't I spend an hour on a single problem during normal study time? The reality is that in fast-paced graduate courses, you often aren't "supposed" to spend that much time stuck on one problem. This is a structural issue with how we learn, and I still don't know if I agree with it or not. An exam setting, however, forces you to sit and dedicate that time strictly to problem-solving.

During regular preparation, you certainly can spend a lot of time, but I usually spend mine diving deep into the underlying theory. Sometimes specific problems just require a specific trick that you either see or you don't. That is not an excuse—I fully accept that I simply didn't figure out the question. But the struggle itself was incredibly fun.

As a final, small remark: it feels like students nowadays lack a strong level of patience. When doing serious mathematics, that lack of patience is really not good for the long term.
